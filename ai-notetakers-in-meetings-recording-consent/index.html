AI Notetakers in Meetings, What Recording Consent Actually Means

A plain explanation of how Zoom and Microsoft Teams implement recording consent, and why some US states legally require everyone's consent to record.

AI at Work, PlainlyOct 9, 2026 · 5 min read

An AI notetaker joining a meeting to record and transcribe everything said is now common enough that most people barely notice the recording icon anymore. But recording a conversation without everyone's knowledge is not just a courtesy issue in some places; it is specifically regulated, both by the meeting software itself and, in some US states, by law. Here is what the actual documentation and legal text say.

How Zoom implements recording consent

Zoom's own support documentation describes a specific, built-in consent flow: "When joining a meeting that is already being recorded or the host begins recording, participants are asked to provide consent." Participants can respond in one of two ways: click OK to grant consent and stay in the meeting, or click Leave to exit without being recorded.

Zoom's documentation also notes that this is not automatically available in full customizable form on every account tier. For Zoom accounts with fewer than 100 licenses, "the recording consent disclaimer is enabled for all users on a Zoom client by default and can't be disabled or customized," though the voice-prompt version of the notification can be turned off. For larger Enterprise, API, Education, or Business accounts with 100 or more licenses, admins can disable the disclaimer entirely for internal participants, while it remains required for all guest participants regardless of account size.

How Microsoft Teams differs: implicit versus explicit consent

Microsoft's documentation for Teams draws a distinction between two consent models. The default is what Microsoft calls implicit consent: a notification banner appears when recording starts, informing participants, but does not require any active response from them to continue in the meeting.

Teams also supports a stricter model called explicit recording consent, specifically for Audio Conferencing. Microsoft's documentation explains the mechanic: "When the explicit recording policy is enabled, all participants are muted once the meeting recording starts. Once a participant decides to un-mute, they're prompted to provide consent." The participant must then actively use their dial pad to confirm consent before they can speak. This explicit mode has to be turned on deliberately by an organization's IT administrator using PowerShell; it is not Teams' default behavior.

This difference matters in practice: a Teams meeting you join could be using either model depending entirely on how your organization's admin has configured it, so the presence of a notification banner alone does not tell you whether the organizer has enabled the stricter explicit-consent requirement.

Why some states require everyone's consent, not just the organizer's

Beyond what the meeting software itself does, several US states have their own legal requirements for recording conversations, independent of any app setting. California is one of the strictest examples. California Penal Code Section 632 states: "A person who, intentionally and without the consent of all parties to a confidential communication, uses an electronic amplifying or recording device to eavesdrop upon or record the confidential communication... shall be punished by a fine not exceeding two thousand five hundred dollars ($2,500) per violation, or imprisonment in a county jail not exceeding one year."

This is commonly called a "two-party" or "all-party" consent law: recording a confidential communication without the consent of every participant can be a criminal offense in California, not merely a breach of workplace etiquette. The law applies specifically to communications where a participant has a reasonable expectation that the conversation is not being recorded, which most ordinary work meetings would qualify as.

Other states follow a "one-party consent" model instead, where only one person on the call (commonly the person doing the recording) needs to consent, which is also the standard under federal wiretapping law. Because state laws vary, and because video calls frequently include participants dialing in from different states, the safest practical assumption for any meeting that might include a California participant, or a participant from another all-party consent state, is that recording requires everyone's awareness and agreement, not just the organizer's.

What this means for AI notetaker tools specifically

An AI notetaker that joins a call to record audio and generate a transcript is, legally and functionally, still "recording" the conversation in the same sense these consent rules and software notifications are built around. If your meeting platform shows a recording consent banner or disclaimer when the notetaker joins, that notification is doing real legal and procedural work, not just appearing as a formality. If a meeting with an AI notetaker does not clearly show who has consented, and the group includes participants in an all-party consent state, that is a genuine compliance question worth raising with whoever scheduled the meeting, not something to assume has already been handled.

A practical checklist before accepting an AI notetaker into a meeting

  • Check which consent model your platform uses. Zoom's default disclaimer requires active consent; Teams' default is a passive notification banner unless explicit consent has been turned on by an admin.
  • Do not assume a recording banner alone satisfies legal requirements in an all-party consent state; the banner informs, but true consent generally requires an affirmative response, not just visibility of a notice.
  • If participants are joining from multiple states, treat the meeting as though the strictest applicable state law applies, since all-party consent states can extend protection to their residents even in a call that includes people from one-party consent states.
  • If you are the organizer, state out loud (or in the invite) that an AI notetaker will be present and recording, rather than relying solely on the software's automated notification.

Key takeaways

  • Zoom's recording consent disclaimer requires an active response (OK or Leave) by default on smaller accounts, while larger accounts can disable it for internal participants only.
  • Microsoft Teams defaults to a passive notification banner (implicit consent); a stricter explicit-consent mode exists for Audio Conferencing but must be turned on by an admin.
  • California Penal Code Section 632 makes recording a confidential communication without the consent of all parties a criminal offense, carrying fines and potential jail time.
  • Some US states require only one party's consent to record, while others, like California, require every participant's consent, so the correct standard depends on who is actually on the call.
  • An AI notetaker is functionally still a recording device, so the same consent notifications and state laws that apply to manual call recording apply to it as well.
